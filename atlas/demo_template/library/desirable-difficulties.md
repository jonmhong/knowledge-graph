## Overview

"Desirable difficulties" is a term coined by the psychologist **Robert Bjork**. It names a pattern that surprises many people: some ways of studying feel slower and harder while you're doing them, but they produce stronger, longer-lasting memory.

## Examples

Several well-studied techniques fit this pattern:

- **Spacing**: spreading reviews out over time instead of cramming.
- **Retrieval practice**: testing yourself instead of re-reading.
- **Interleaving**: mixing different kinds of problems instead of practicing one kind at a time.
- **Generation**: trying to come up with an answer before you're shown it.

Each one adds effort or mistakes during practice, and each tends to improve recall later.

## Performance Versus Learning

Bjork and colleagues separate two things:

- **Performance** is how well you do during practice.
- **Learning** is the lasting change that shows up later.

The two can move in opposite directions. Easy, fluent practice raises today's performance but does little for learning. Effortful practice can do the reverse.

## Not Every Difficulty Is Desirable

The word "desirable" is doing real work here. A difficulty helps only if the learner can still overcome it with effort. Material that is simply too advanced, confusing instructions, or a noisy room make learning harder without making it better. The helpful kind of difficulty makes you work at *remembering or deciding*, not at *deciphering*.

## Why People Avoid Them

Effortful methods feel less successful, so learners and teachers often judge them to be worse. Knowing about desirable difficulties helps you trust a method that feels clumsy but works.
