## Overview

Deliberate practice is a specific kind of practice meant to improve performance. It is not the same as repeating something you can already do. The psychologist **K. Anders Ericsson** described it in his research on expert musicians, athletes and chess players.

## Features of deliberate practice

According to Ericsson's work, deliberate practice usually has four features:

- **A specific goal aimed at a weakness.** For example, a musician works on one difficult passage instead of playing the whole piece again.
- **Full concentration.** It is mentally demanding, so sessions tend to be fairly short.
- **Immediate feedback**, often from a teacher or coach, so mistakes are noticed and corrected.
- **Working just beyond current ability**, where success is possible but not guaranteed.

## Why experience alone is not enough

Many people do an activity for years without getting much better. Once a skill becomes automatic, ordinary repetition mostly keeps it at the same level. Improvement tends to stall unless practice is changed to target the parts that are still hard.

## The debate over how much practice explains

Ericsson's research became popular as the "10,000-hour rule." Ericsson himself disputed that phrase. Later studies found that the amount of practice explains a meaningful but limited share of the differences between people, and that this share varies a lot between fields. Practice matters a great deal, but it is not the only factor.

## Connection to memory research

Deliberate practice shares a theme with research on **desirable difficulties**: effective practice is usually uncomfortable. Going after the hard parts, getting feedback and correcting errors is slower and less pleasant than repeating what already works. The source argues that this discomfort is exactly why it leads to improvement.
