## Origin: Ebbinghaus's self-experiment

In the 1880s, German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus ran memory tests on himself. He memorized long lists of nonsense syllables (such as "DAX" or "BOK") and then measured how much he could recall after different delays. He picked meaningless syllables on purpose so that nothing he already knew could help him remember them.

## The shape of the curve

What he found is now called the **forgetting curve**. Forgetting happens fastest right after learning and then slows down. A large share of new material is lost within the first day or two. Whatever survives that early drop fades much more slowly afterwards.

## What makes the curve steeper or flatter

- **Meaning:** Material that connects to things you already know is forgotten far more slowly than arbitrary facts. This is why Ebbinghaus chose nonsense syllables: they removed that advantage.
- **Strength of the first learning:** Being able to recall something once is not the same as recalling it easily. Practicing beyond the first successful recall flattens the curve.
- **Review:** Each successful recall makes the next decline slower. This effect is the basis of **spaced repetition**.

## Savings

Ebbinghaus also found an effect he called **savings**. Even when he could no longer recall a list at all, relearning it took less time than learning it the first time. Forgetting is not the same as erasing: some trace of the memory stays and makes the second attempt cheaper.

## Practical takeaway

A single study session, however intense, is a weak way to hold onto information. Forgetting can't be avoided entirely, so the real question is how to space reviews so the curve flattens with as little effort as possible.
