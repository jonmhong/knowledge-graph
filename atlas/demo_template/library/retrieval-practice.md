## Overview

We usually treat tests as a way to *measure* learning. Research in cognitive psychology suggests they are also one of the best ways to *produce* it. **Retrieval practice** means deliberately pulling information out of memory instead of putting it in again by re-reading. You can do this by answering questions, using flashcards, or writing down everything you remember.

## The Testing Effect

Psychologists Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke ran a well-known series of studies comparing two groups of students:

- One group **re-read** a passage several times.
- The other group **read it once, then practiced recalling** it.

Right after studying, the re-readers often did about as well as the recall group, or better. Days later, though, the students who had practiced recall remembered considerably more. This long-term advantage of recalling over re-reading is called the **testing effect**.

## Why Re-reading Feels Better Than It Is

Re-reading makes the text feel familiar. Because the text looks known, it seems learned. But *recognizing* something that is in front of you is very different from *producing* it from memory. Retrieval practice is harder and less pleasant, which is part of why people underuse it. That difficulty is also what makes it work.

## Practical Techniques

- Close the book, write a summary from memory, then check what you missed.
- Turn headings into questions and answer them before reading each section.
- Use flashcards, and say the answer out loud before flipping the card.
- Explain the idea to someone else without notes.

## Relationship to Spaced Repetition

Retrieval practice pairs naturally with spaced repetition. Spacing decides **when** to review, and retrieval decides **how** to review.
