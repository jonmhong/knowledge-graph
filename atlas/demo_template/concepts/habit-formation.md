title: Habit Formation
---

A habit is something you do automatically, without stopping to decide. It is set off by a familiar situation rather than a deliberate choice. Habit formation is how a behavior goes from needing conscious effort to running on its own. Brushing your teeth before bed and reaching for your phone when you sit down are everyday examples. Understanding how habits form helps you build behaviors you want and break ones you don't. ([[library/how-habits-form|How Habits Form: The Habit Loop, Timelines, and Practical Strategies]])

## The Habit Loop

Habits are commonly described as a loop with three parts ([[library/how-habits-form|How Habits Form]]):

1. **Cue**: something that triggers the behavior. It can come from your surroundings or your routine, such as a time of day, a place, or a feeling.
2. **Routine**: the behavior itself.
3. **Reward**: something satisfying that follows, which makes the brain more likely to repeat the loop.

When the behavior is repeated in a stable context, the link between cue and routine gets stronger. Eventually the behavior happens with little thought.

## How Long Habit Formation Takes

Research does not support the popular claim that habits take 21 days to form. In a frequently cited study, Phillippa Lally and colleagues found that participants needed anywhere from a few weeks to many months before a new daily behavior felt automatic. The time varied a lot between people and between behaviors. Missing a single day did not seem to derail the process. ([[library/how-habits-form|How Habits Form]])

## Building and Breaking Habits

Practical strategies drawn from the habit loop ([[library/how-habits-form|How Habits Form]]):

- **Attach new habits to existing cues.** Tie a new behavior to a reliable trigger you already have, e.g. "After I pour my morning coffee, I will review my flashcards."
- **Start very small.** A tiny version of the behavior is easier to repeat consistently, and consistency is what makes a behavior automatic.
- **Change the environment.** Removing a cue often works better than relying on willpower. Keeping your phone in another room is one example.

## Habits and Learning

Study methods like spaced repetition only work if reviews actually happen regularly. People who stick with a learning plan often differ from those who don't in one way: they have made review a habit tied to a daily cue. ([[library/how-habits-form|How Habits Form]])
