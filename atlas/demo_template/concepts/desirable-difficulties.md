---
title: Desirable Difficulties
---

Desirable difficulties are ways of practicing that feel harder or slower while you do them but help you remember and use what you learned for longer. People often expect smooth, easy studying to work best. Often it doesn't: a certain amount of effort during practice makes memory stronger, as long as you can still get past the difficulty.

## Origin of the Term

The psychologist **Robert Bjork** coined the term "desirable difficulties." It describes a surprising pattern: some study methods feel less effective in the moment but produce stronger, longer-lasting memory ([[library/desirable-difficulties|Desirable Difficulties: When Harder Learning Sticks Better]]).

## Common Examples

Several well-studied techniques fit the pattern ([[library/desirable-difficulties|Desirable Difficulties: When Harder Learning Sticks Better]]):

- **Spacing**: spreading reviews out over time instead of cramming.
- **Retrieval practice**: testing yourself instead of re-reading.
- **Interleaving**: mixing different kinds of problems instead of practicing one kind at a time.
- **Generation**: trying to come up with an answer before you're shown it.

Each one adds effort or mistakes during practice, and each tends to improve recall later.

## Performance Versus Learning

Bjork and colleagues separate two things that are easy to confuse ([[library/desirable-difficulties|Desirable Difficulties: When Harder Learning Sticks Better]]):

- **Performance** is how well you do during practice.
- **Learning** is the lasting change that shows up later.

These two can move in opposite directions. Easy, fluent practice raises today's performance but does little for learning. Effortful practice can lower today's performance while improving learning.

## Not Every Difficulty Is Desirable

The word "desirable" matters. A difficulty helps only if the learner can still overcome it with effort. Material that is simply too advanced, confusing instructions, or a noisy room make learning harder without making it better. The useful kind of difficulty makes you work at *remembering or deciding*, not at *deciphering* ([[library/desirable-difficulties|Desirable Difficulties: When Harder Learning Sticks Better]]).

## Why People Avoid Them

Effortful methods feel less successful, so learners and teachers often judge them to be worse than they are. Knowing about desirable difficulties helps you trust a method that feels clumsy but works ([[library/desirable-difficulties|Desirable Difficulties: When Harder Learning Sticks Better]]).
