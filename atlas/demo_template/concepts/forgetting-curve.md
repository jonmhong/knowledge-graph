---
title: Forgetting Curve
---

The **forgetting curve** describes how new information fades from memory over time if you do nothing to keep it. You lose a lot right after learning something, and then the forgetting slows down. Knowing this helps explain why a single study session rarely makes something stick, and why well-timed reviews work so much better ([[library/the-forgetting-curve|The Forgetting Curve: Why We Lose What We Learn]]).

## Origin: Ebbinghaus's self-experiment

The idea comes from experiments that the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus ran on himself in the 1880s. He memorized long lists of nonsense syllables such as "DAX" or "BOK." Then he measured how much he could still recall after different delays. He chose meaningless syllables on purpose, so that nothing he already knew could help him remember them. That let him measure forgetting in its "pure" form ([[library/the-forgetting-curve|The Forgetting Curve: Why We Lose What We Learn]]).

## The shape of the curve

Forgetting is fastest right after learning and then slows down. A large share of new material is lost within the first day or two. Whatever survives that early drop fades much more slowly afterwards ([[library/the-forgetting-curve|The Forgetting Curve: Why We Lose What We Learn]]).

## What makes the curve steeper or flatter

The curve isn't fixed. Several factors change how fast something is forgotten ([[library/the-forgetting-curve|The Forgetting Curve: Why We Lose What We Learn]]):

- **Meaning:** Material that connects to things you already know is forgotten far more slowly than arbitrary facts. This is exactly the advantage that Ebbinghaus's nonsense syllables were designed to remove.
- **Strength of the first learning:** Being able to recall something once is not the same as recalling it easily. Practicing past the first successful recall (sometimes called overlearning) flattens the curve.
- **Review:** Each successful recall makes the next decline slower. This is the basis of **spaced repetition**, where reviews are timed to come just as memory starts to fade.

## Savings: forgetting is not erasing

Ebbinghaus also found an effect he called **savings**. Even when he could no longer recall a list at all, relearning it took less time than learning it the first time. So something that seems completely forgotten still leaves a trace in memory, and that trace makes the second attempt cheaper ([[library/the-forgetting-curve|The Forgetting Curve: Why We Lose What We Learn]]).

## Practical takeaway

A single study session is a weak way to hold onto information, however intense it is. Forgetting can't be avoided entirely, so the useful question is how to space reviews so the curve flattens with as little total effort as possible ([[library/the-forgetting-curve|The Forgetting Curve: Why We Lose What We Learn]]).
