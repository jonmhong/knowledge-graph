title: Deliberate Practice
---

Deliberate practice is a way of practicing that is built to make you better at something, not just to keep you where you are. You don't repeat what you can already do comfortably. You pick out the specific things you still do poorly, work on them with full attention, find out right away what went wrong, and adjust. It answers a common puzzle: why do many people do an activity for years and barely improve, while others keep getting better? [[library/deliberate-practice|Deliberate Practice: How Experts Get Better]]

## Origins

The term comes from the psychologist **K. Anders Ericsson**, who studied how expert musicians, athletes and chess players became so skilled. He found that their improvement came from a particular kind of focused, effortful practice, not from simply putting in time. [[library/deliberate-practice|Deliberate Practice: How Experts Get Better]]

## Core features

According to Ericsson's work, deliberate practice usually has four features [[library/deliberate-practice|Deliberate Practice: How Experts Get Better]]:

- **A specific goal aimed at a weakness.** For example, a musician works on one difficult passage instead of playing the whole piece again.
- **Full concentration.** It is mentally demanding, so sessions tend to be fairly short.
- **Immediate feedback**, often from a teacher or coach, so mistakes are noticed and corrected.
- **Working just beyond current ability**, where success is possible but not guaranteed.

## Why experience alone is not enough

Many people do an activity for years without getting much better. Once a skill becomes automatic, ordinary repetition mostly keeps it at the same level. Improvement tends to stall unless practice is changed to target the parts that are still hard. This is the main difference between deliberate practice and plain experience: experience builds up on its own, but improvement has to be aimed at something. ## How much practice explains: the "10,000-hour" debate

Ericsson's research became widely known as the "10,000-hour rule," a phrase Ericsson himself disputed. Later studies found that the amount of practice explains a meaningful but limited share of the differences between people, and that this share varies a lot from one field to another. Practice matters a great deal, but it is not the only factor. [[library/deliberate-practice|Deliberate Practice: How Experts Get Better]]

## Connection to desirable difficulties

Deliberate practice shares a theme with memory research on **desirable difficulties**: effective practice is usually uncomfortable. Going after the hard parts, getting feedback and correcting errors is slower and less pleasant than repeating what already works. The source argues that this discomfort is exactly why it leads to improvement. [[library/deliberate-practice|Deliberate Practice: How Experts Get Better]]
