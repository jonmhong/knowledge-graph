# How sleep helps you remember

Sleep is not just rest for the body. It plays an active part in turning the day's experiences into lasting memories, a process known as memory consolidation.

## What happens overnight

While you are awake, new information is held in a fragile form. During sleep, and especially during deep slow-wave sleep, the brain appears to replay recent experiences. This replay is thought to help move memories from short-term storage in the hippocampus into more stable, long-term networks in the cortex. Dream-rich REM sleep has been linked to other kinds of learning, such as skills and emotional memories, although the details are still being studied.

## Evidence from everyday learning

People who sleep after learning something typically remember it better the next day than people who stay awake for the same amount of time. A short nap can produce a smaller version of the same benefit. Sleep also seems to help people notice hidden patterns or rules in material they studied the day before.

## Sleep loss works the other way

Going without enough sleep makes it harder to take in new information the next day: attention drifts and new memories form less reliably. This is one reason all-night cramming before an exam often backfires. The extra hours of study are paid for with worse encoding and weaker consolidation.

## Practical takeaways

- Study the most important material on days when you can get a full night's sleep afterwards.
- Spread learning across several days so that several nights of sleep can help consolidate it.
- Treat sleep as part of studying, not time taken away from it.
