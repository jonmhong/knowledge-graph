# Deliberate practice: how experts get better

Deliberate practice is a specific kind of practice aimed at improving performance, described by the psychologist K. Anders Ericsson in his research on expert musicians, athletes and chess players. It is different from simply repeating an activity you already know how to do.

## What makes practice deliberate

According to Ericsson's work, deliberate practice usually has these features:

- **A specific goal** that targets a weakness, such as one difficult passage of music rather than playing the whole piece again.
- **Full concentration.** It is mentally demanding, so sessions tend to be limited in length.
- **Immediate feedback**, often from a teacher or coach, so mistakes are noticed and corrected.
- **Working just beyond current ability**, where success is possible but not guaranteed.

## Why experience alone is not enough

Many people do an activity for years without getting much better. Once a skill becomes automatic, ordinary repetition mostly maintains it. Improvement tends to stall unless practice is redesigned to target what is still hard.

## The debate

Ericsson's research was popularized as a "10,000-hour rule", a phrase he himself disputed. Later studies have found that the amount of practice explains a meaningful but limited share of the differences between people, and that this share varies a lot between fields. Practice matters a great deal, but it is not the only factor.

## Connection to memory research

Deliberate practice shares a theme with research on desirable difficulties: effective practice is usually uncomfortable. Seeking out the hard parts, getting feedback and correcting errors is slower and less pleasant than repeating what already works, and that is why it produces improvement.
