# How habits form

A habit is a behavior that has become automatic: it is triggered by a situation rather than by a conscious decision. Brushing your teeth before bed or checking your phone when you sit down are typical examples.

## The habit loop

A common way to describe habits is as a loop with three parts:

1. **Cue**: a trigger in the environment or in your routine, such as a time of day, a place, or a feeling.
2. **Routine**: the behavior itself.
3. **Reward**: something satisfying that follows, which makes the brain more likely to repeat the loop next time.

With repetition in a stable context, the link between cue and routine strengthens until the behavior happens with little thought.

## How long it takes

A popular claim says habits take 21 days to form. Research does not support a single number. In one frequently cited study by Phillippa Lally and colleagues, participants took anywhere from a few weeks to many months for a new daily behavior to feel automatic, with large differences between people and between behaviors. Missing a single day did not seem to ruin the process.

## Building and breaking habits

- **Attach new habits to existing cues.** "After I pour my morning coffee, I will review my flashcards" uses a reliable trigger that already exists.
- **Start very small.** A tiny version of the behavior is easier to repeat consistently, and consistency is what builds automaticity.
- **Change the environment.** Removing the cue, such as keeping the phone in another room, is often more effective than relying on willpower.

## Habits and learning

Study methods such as spaced repetition only work if reviews actually happen regularly. Turning review into a habit tied to a daily cue is often what separates people who stick with a learning plan from those who do not.
