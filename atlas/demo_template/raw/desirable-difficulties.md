# Desirable difficulties: when harder learning sticks better

"Desirable difficulties" is a term coined by the psychologist Robert Bjork. It describes a surprising pattern: some ways of studying that make learning feel slower and harder in the moment actually lead to stronger, longer-lasting memory.

## Examples

Several well-studied techniques fall into this category:

- **Spacing** reviews out over time instead of cramming.
- **Retrieval practice**, meaning testing yourself instead of re-reading.
- **Interleaving** different kinds of problems instead of practicing one kind at a time.
- **Generation**, where you try to come up with an answer before being shown it.

Each of these introduces effort or mistakes during practice, and each tends to improve later recall.

## Performance versus learning

Bjork and colleagues draw a distinction between *performance*, meaning how well you do during practice, and *learning*, meaning the lasting change that shows up later. The two can point in opposite directions. Easy, fluent practice boosts performance today while doing little for learning, and effortful practice can do the reverse.

## Not every difficulty is desirable

The word "desirable" matters. A difficulty helps only if the learner can still overcome it with effort. Material that is simply too advanced, confusing instructions, or a noisy room make learning harder without making it better. The useful kind of difficulty makes you work at remembering or deciding, not at deciphering.

## Why people avoid them

Because effortful methods feel less successful, learners and teachers often judge them to be worse. Knowing about desirable difficulties helps you trust a method that feels clumsy but works.
