# Interleaving: mixing up what you practice

When people practice a skill, they usually do it in blocks: twenty problems of one type, then twenty of the next. Interleaving means mixing different types of problems or skills within the same session instead, for example alternating between three kinds of math problems rather than finishing all of one kind first.

## Why mixing helps

Blocked practice tells you which method to use before you start, because every problem in the block needs the same one. In real life, and on most exams, nobody tells you that. Interleaving forces you to decide which approach a problem needs, and learning to tell problem types apart turns out to be a large part of mastering a subject.

Studies with students learning mathematics, and with people learning to tell apart painters' styles or bird species, have found that interleaved practice usually leads to better performance on later tests, even though it feels harder at the time.

## The feeling of fluency is misleading

During blocked practice, performance improves quickly within the session, which feels like progress. Interleaved practice produces more mistakes and slower improvement in the moment. Learners often prefer blocking and believe they learn more from it, even when tests show the opposite. This mismatch between how learning feels and how well it works appears again and again in memory research.

## When to use it

Interleaving works best once you have a basic grasp of each skill. Mixing topics you have never seen before can just be confusing. A sensible pattern is to learn a new method in a short block, then fold it into mixed practice with older material.
