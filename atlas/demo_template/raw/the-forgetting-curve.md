# The forgetting curve: why we lose what we learn

In the 1880s the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus ran an unusual experiment on himself. He memorized long lists of nonsense syllables, such as "DAX" or "BOK", chosen so that no prior meaning could help him, and then tested how much he could still recall after different delays.

The pattern he found is now called the forgetting curve. Memory loss is steepest right after learning and then slows down. A large share of new material is gone within the first day or two, but whatever survives that early drop tends to fade much more gradually afterwards.

## What makes the curve steeper or flatter

Several things change how fast something is forgotten:

- **Meaning.** Material that connects to things you already know is forgotten far more slowly than arbitrary facts. Ebbinghaus used nonsense syllables precisely to remove this advantage.
- **Strength of the first learning.** Studying until you can recall something once is not the same as studying until recall is easy. Extra practice beyond the first success flattens the curve.
- **Review.** Each time a memory is successfully brought back, the next decline is slower. This is the foundation of spaced repetition.

## Savings

One of Ebbinghaus's subtler findings was "savings". Even when he could no longer recall a list at all, relearning it took less time than learning it the first time. Forgotten is not the same as erased; some trace remains, and it makes the second attempt cheaper.

## Why it matters

The forgetting curve is a reminder that a single study session, however intense, is a weak way to hold onto information. The practical question is not how to avoid forgetting entirely, since that is impossible, but how to arrange reviews so the curve flattens with as little effort as possible.
