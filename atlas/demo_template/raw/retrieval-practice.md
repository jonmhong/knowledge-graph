# Retrieval practice: testing as a way to learn

Most people think of tests as a way to measure learning. Research in cognitive psychology suggests they are also one of the best ways to produce it. Retrieval practice means deliberately pulling information out of memory, by answering questions, using flashcards, or writing down everything you remember, rather than putting information in by re-reading.

## The testing effect

In a well-known line of studies, psychologists Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke compared students who re-read a passage several times with students who read it once and then practiced recalling it. Right after studying, the re-readers often did about as well or better. But days later, the students who had practiced recall remembered considerably more. This advantage of recalling over re-reading is known as the testing effect.

## Why re-reading feels better than it is

Re-reading creates a feeling of familiarity. The text looks known, so it seems learned. But recognizing something when it is in front of you is very different from producing it from memory. Retrieval practice is harder and feels less pleasant, which is part of why people underuse it, yet that difficulty is what makes it effective.

## Simple ways to use it

- Close the book and write a summary from memory, then check what you missed.
- Turn headings into questions and answer them before reading the section.
- Use flashcards, and say the answer out loud before flipping the card.
- Explain the idea to someone else without notes.

Retrieval practice pairs naturally with spaced repetition: spacing decides *when* to review, and retrieval decides *how*.
